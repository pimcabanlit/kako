# General guidance

Kako's general guidance for any household (synthetic demo). These are common rules of thumb, not
financial advice and not the household's own figures: Kako's engine works those out from the
household's facts. Rules of thumb differ between sources; use them as a starting point.

## Emergency fund

- An emergency fund is money kept for a loss of income or an urgent cost, in an account the
  household can reach quickly.
- A common rule of thumb is 3 to 6 months of essential expenses. A household with one income, or
  income that changes month to month, often aims for the upper end.
- The runway is how many months the money in liquid accounts would cover essential expenses with no
  income at all.

## Budgeting

- The 50/30/20 rule of thumb splits take-home pay into 50% for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for
  savings and paying down debt.
- Essential expenses are what the household must pay each month: housing, food, utilities,
  transport, school, insurance and minimum debt payments.
- Spending on wants (eating out, subscriptions, shopping) is the easiest to change. Whether an
  amount is a lot depends on the household's surplus and goals, not on a fixed number.
- A sinking fund sets money aside each month for a known cost later in the year, such as school
  fees or a big repair, so it doesn't come from the emergency fund.

## Debt

- Interest on a credit card is charged on the balance left unpaid after the due date. Paying the
  full statement balance by the due date avoids interest; paying only the minimum keeps the debt
  for much longer.
- Philippine credit cards often charge up to about 3% a month on an unpaid balance.
- The avalanche method pays extra on the debt with the highest interest rate first, and costs the
  least interest. The snowball method pays extra on the smallest balance first, and clears a debt
  sooner, which some households find motivating.
- A common rule of thumb is to keep total monthly debt payments below about 30% of take-home pay;
  lenders may look at a similar share before approving a new loan.
- Before taking a loan, compare the total cost: the interest over the whole term and any fees, not
  only the monthly payment.

## Credit cards and rewards

- A cashback or rewards card pays back a small share of spending. It helps only when the balance is
  paid in full every month: interest on an unpaid balance is usually far more than the rewards.
- Annual fees, a higher spending habit and a second card's minimum payments can cancel out the
  rewards. A household paying down card debt usually gains more from clearing it first.

## Saving and investing

- Money needed within a year or two usually stays in a savings account or a time deposit, where its
  value doesn't move with markets.
- For longer goals, kinds of investment include government bonds, pooled funds (mutual funds and
  UITFs) and shares. Higher possible returns come with a higher chance of losing money in a bad
  year.
- Before investing, households usually build the emergency fund and pay down high-interest debt
  first, since card interest costs more than most investments earn.
- Kako explains kinds of investment and their trade-offs. It never picks or names a product, and has
  no live prices or rates.

## Insurance

- Health cover (PhilHealth, and an HMO through work) and life insurance for an earner with
  dependants protect the emergency fund from a large cost.
- Insurance is for protection; mixing it with investing often costs more in fees.

## Tax

- Kako explains how tax works from its reference documents, such as 13th-month pay. It never works
  out a tax amount: for that, the household checks with its employer or a tax adviser.
